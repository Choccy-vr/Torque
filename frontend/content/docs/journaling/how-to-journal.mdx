A journal entry is a quick write-up of a chunk of work, backed by a photo or two and the time you spent. 

In Torque, you post journal entries directly on the website as you build. Your journals do three major things:
1. **Logs your time:** Captures the hours you spent designing, fabricating, or soldering.
2. **Maintains your streak:** Keeps your daily streak alive to maximize your Volts multiplier.
3. **Serves as proof:** Acts as the primary evidence reviewers evaluate when approving grant hours and shipping your build.

<Callout type="info" title="Journaling on the Web + Export to Markdown">
  You will write and publish your entries directly using the **Torque web dashboard**. 
  
  Whenever you want a permanent copy for your GitHub repository or personal portfolio, click **Export JOURNAL.md** from your dashboard to download your entire log as a clean Markdown file with frontmatter ready to drop into your repo root!
</Callout>

## What Goes into an Entry?

When submitting a journal entry on the platform, each post requires:

| Field | Requirement | Details |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Title** | Required (≤ 75 chars) | A punchy summary of what you tackled (e.g., *"Tuning PID loop on the pitch axis"*). |
| **Content** | Required (≥ 100 chars) | An honest breakdown of what you did, why you did it, and what roadblocks you hit. |
| **Media / Photos** | Required | Clear photos or screenshots showing work-in-progress intermediates. |
| **Hours Logged** | Required (0.1–20 hrs) | The time spent during that specific session. If a build session exceeds 20 hours, break it up. |

<Callout type="error" title="Strictly No AI-Generated Journals">
  Reviewers read every journal. AI-generated entries are obvious, lack authentic troubleshooting details, and are grounds for **immediate rejection**. Honest, messy, and brief human writing always beats polished robotic fluff.
</Callout>

## How to Write a Solid Entry

A great journal entry reads like a build log, not a sterile checklist. Answer three questions every time:
1. **What did you build or change?**
2. **Why did you choose that approach?**
3. **What failed, and how did you resolve it?**

### The Hardware Evidence Ratio
Hardware projects live and die by visual proof:
* Aim for roughly **one photo or screenshot per 3–5 hours of work**.
* A single picture of a closed plastic box cannot verify 15 hours of internal wiring. Show the messy wiring harness, the misaligned motor mounts, the burned driver boards, and the scope traces.
* **Videos & Timelapses:** For testing mechanisms or showing motor motion, upload short clips or link unlisted YouTube timelapses directly in your entry body.

## Real Examples: Bad vs. Good

<Tabs items={["Bad Example", "Good Example"]}>
  <Tab value="Bad Example">
    ```markdown
    I added a buck converter and wired it up. I did CAD and added a case. 
    I soldered everything together and it worked!
    ```

    **Why reviewers bounce this back:**

    - *Why* did you need a buck converter? What voltage are you stepping down from/to?
    - What mounting features or clearances did the CAD case require?
    - How did you test the motor under load?
    - There is zero insight into your engineering choices.
  </Tab>
  <Tab value="Good Example">
    ```markdown
    ### Got the closed-loop encoder reading correctly!

    I spent the afternoon wiring the AS5600 magnetic encoder to the rear of the BLDC gimbal motor. 
    Originally, the I2C bus was hanging whenever the motor spun up. After probing the lines, 
    I realized the motor phase wires were routed right next to the SDA/SCL lines, inducing 
    massive inductive noise.

    I redesigned the 3D-printed end-cap to route the encoder ribbon cable out the back through 
    a separate channel and added external 4.7kΩ pull-up resistors on the 3.3V rail. Once flashed, 
    the angle readings stayed stable at 1000 RPM!
    ```

    **Why this passes:**

    - Explains the exact problem (I2C bus hanging from inductive motor noise).
    - Documents the physical iteration (rerouted cables + redesigned 3D mount + pull-ups).
    - Shows tangible proof of progress.
  </Tab>
</Tabs>

## Dos and Don'ts

| Do | Don't |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Log frequently:** One entry per day or milestone keeps your streak intact. | **Batch dumps:** Waiting two weeks and dumping a single 30-hour entry without photos. |
| **Document failures:** Burned out a driver? Stripped a gear? Tell the story! | **Hide mistakes:** Submitting only the final pristine product looks like a kit build. |
| **Show intermediate progress:** Screenshot CAD sketches, wiring diagrams, and test benches. | **Vague summaries:** Writing *"worked on code"* or *"did some 3D modeling"*. |
| **Write like yourself:** Grammatical perfection doesn't matter; authentic details do. | **Use LLMs to summarize:** AI-generated text will flag review filters. |
