A good project is something you **genuinely wanted to build**, executed well enough that you would proudly show it off anywhere. That is what makes a good project.

You shouldn't just be building something to claim a grant or hit an hour requirement. By the time you ship, your project should feel intentional, functional, and uniquely yours.

---

## What "Good" Really Means

The strongest hardware projects start with genuine curiosity or a concrete frustration:
* *"I want an automated blind puller because morning light wakes me up at 6 AM."*
* *"I want an RC drift trike with a custom steering linkage because commercial kits are boring."*
* *"I saw an inverted pendulum on YouTube and wanted to tune my own PID control loop."*

If someone searches your name two years from now and finds your build log, repository, and demo video, you should be proud of it.

<Callout type="warn" title="Ask yourself: Why am I building this?">
  If your only answer is *"I wanted to earn Volts"* or *"I just wanted free parts,"* pause and rethink the concept. The best builds come from wanting the physical mechanism to exist in the real world.
</Callout>

---

## The Difference at a Glance

| Quality | What we love to see | What falls short |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Origin** | An idea built to solve a problem or explore a mechanism | Cloned schematics or copy-pasted 3D models with zero changes |
| **Execution** | Secure wiring, thought-out mounting, tuned motion | Dangling Dupont wires, hot-glued motors, loose gears |
| **Identity** | Distinct name, documented purpose, real demo | Vague concepts (*"Smart AI Car"*, *"IoT Motor"*) |
| **Scope** | Small, dialed-in mechanism with polish | Massive sci-fi concept that barely turns on |

## The Three Pillars of a Great Build

### 1. Be Specific
Broad ideas lead to half-baked projects. Specific constraints make engineering fun:
* **Vague:** *"A smart motorized lock."*
* **Specific:** *"A deadbolt actuator triggered by an RFID ring, with a physical manual override lever."*
* **Vague:** *"An RC rover."*
* **Specific:** *"A dual-motor differential drive rover using a custom laser-cut chassis to map my hallway."*

### 2. Make It Feel Finished
Hardware doesn't need to look factory-molded, but it must look **intentional**:

- **Route your wiring:** Secure wire runs with zip ties, heat shrink, or 3D-printed clips. Loose jumper wires rattling next to rotating pinions cause short circuits and just don't look good.
- **Rigid mounting:** Design brackets or print mounts. Motors exert real torque—double-sided tape and hot glue won't hold under stall loads. Your Project should last.
- **Proper documentation:** A clear `README.md` with a bill of materials (BOM), wiring diagram/schematic, pictures, and a short video demo video.

### 3. Build It Yourself
You do not need to invent brushless motors or write PID algorithms from scratch. However:

<Callout type="error" title="Never submit direct clones">
  Downloading someone else's complete CAD assembly and 3D printing it is **not** a good project. Reviewers check this. If you rely heavily on reference designs or libraries, give explicit credit in your README and clearly explain **what parts you engineered yourself**.
</Callout>

## Avoiding Generic Builds (And How to Fix Them)

Most beginner hardware traps suffer from lack of personality or overscoping. Here is how to upgrade common starting points:

<Tabs items={['Motorized Trap', 'The Upgrade']}>
  <Tab value="Motorized Trap">
    * **The Stock Arduino Robot:** A generic blue 2WD chassis kit running boilerplate sweep code.
    * **The Basic Servo Alarm:** An SG90 micro servo taped to a light switch with no enclosure.
    * **The "Smart" Fan:** A relay and DC motor that just turns on when room temp hits 75°F.
    * **The Ambiguous "AI Robot":** An oversized machine trying to do computer vision, robotic arms, and speech before the wheels even spin.
  </Tab>
  <Tab value="The Upgrade">
    * **Custom Drivetrain:** Design your own plate chassis in CAD, tune proper motor drivers, and add encoder feedback for straight-line tracking.
    * **Mechanical Whack-a-Mole:** Mount the alarm in an enclosure where turning it off requires knocking down mechanical targets driven by solenoids.
    * **Oscillating Air Cannon:** Use a geared stepper to sweep angles, custom ducting, and dynamic speed profiling based on distance.
    * **Scope Small, Nail the Polish:** Ditch the vague "AI" tag. Make a rock-solid line follower or a self-balancing pendulum where the mechanical tolerances are dialed in.
  </Tab>
</Tabs>
